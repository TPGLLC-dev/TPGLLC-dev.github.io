Parks are fun. Lines aren't.

Why a family of passholders built an app to decide how long is too long to wait, and to tell you when the wait gets there.

How it started

Late in 2025 we were standing in the Jungle Cruise line at Magic Kingdom, and it felt long. Was it, though? Was this a normal wait for that ride at that hour, or a bad one? There was no ride history to check, so there was no way to tell. We thought about stepping out, stayed, and rode.

Afterward we kept coming back to one idea: what if you decided how long is too long, and your phone or watch told you when the wait got there? No refreshing an app all day. The parks' own apps are good, but there were things we wanted that they don't do: a wait you set yourself, an alert the moment a broken-down ride reopens, and a plan that changes when the day does.

So in early 2026 we started recording every Walt Disney World ride's wait and status, every two minutes, all day, and we haven't stopped. That record became MagiPulse, which reached the App Store in July 2026. The same approach now covers Universal Orlando, SeaWorld and Busch Gardens, and Disneyland, and it carried over to days outside the parks: BeachPulse picks a Florida beach on live weather, water and drive time, and LinksPulse does the same for Central Florida golf.

Why we record every wait

A live wait tells you about right now. To know whether 45 minutes is normal for a ride, or what it will be at 3 p.m., you need history. That's why we record every ride's wait every two minutes, and why the record keeps growing.

Every night, that record retrains our forecast models. One of them is a neural network, a kind of machine learning that finds patterns too tangled to write down by hand. The models learn from things like the hour, the day of the week and the time of year; the weather (heat, humidity, the chance of rain); the ride itself (indoor or outdoor, thrill or gentle, how popular it is); and special nights, like the Halloween and Christmas parties that close Magic Kingdom early to day guests.

We keep checking the work. Each morning, yesterday's forecast is scored against what really happened, and we look for ways to make the data behind it better. In June 2026 we tightened the data the models learn from. Once we had enough party nights to learn from, party nights got their own correction too.

So how close is it? Since that change (June 20 to October 21, 2026, at Walt Disney World), gentle rides like Dumbo and the Mad Tea Party have landed within 10 minutes of the forecast more than 9 times in 10. Mid-size rides have landed within 10 minutes about 8 times in 10. Headliners like TRON and Rise of the Resistance are the hardest: within 10 minutes a little over half the time, and within 15 minutes about 3 times in 4. Across every ride, 79% of forecasts landed within 10 minutes.

Headliners are harder because one breakdown or a passing storm can add an hour to the line, and a forecast made the night before can't see that coming. That's why the app also watches live. It tells you when a wait drops to your number, or the moment a broken-down ride reopens.

Who they're for

Every kind of park day. The app should help with yours.

First-time visitors

Not sure what to do next? It shows which rides are short right now and which are worth waiting for.

Planners

Every minute mapped out before you arrive? Your plan adjusts when a ride goes down or a wait jumps.

Passholders who stroll

A few must-dos and no schedule? Set the wait you'll accept and get an alert when it's there.

How we test it

We'd rather show nothing than a wrong number. So every part of the app is checked against what actually happened.

2 min

Live, all day

Waits and ride status refresh every two minutes while the parks are open.

Every night

Forecasts scored

Each night, yesterday's forecast is checked against the real waits. At Walt Disney World it has been off by about 7 minutes on a typical day.

6 a.m.

A daily health check

Before the parks open, a set of checks looks for anything that stopped updating or looks wrong. Most problems are caught before the first guest opens the app.

In the parks

Tested on foot

Living near the parks means we test there. Features that use your location have been through dozens of field tests, on foot, not just in a simulator.

60 days

Replayed before it ships

New planning features are replayed against real park days first. Saving one ride for the last hour before close saved about 20 minutes of waiting per day across 60 replayed days, so it shipped.

About 22

Why reopening alerts matter

On a typical day at Walt Disney World, about 22 attractions go down for 10 minutes or more. A headliner went down on every one of the last 50 days we counted.

What we won't do

  • Pretend to be the parks. We're independent. Disney, Universal and SeaWorld don't make, endorse or sponsor these apps.
  • Reword a weather warning. Official National Weather Service alerts appear word for word, labeled as theirs. If the weather service can't be reached, the app says so instead of showing an all-clear.
  • Track you on this site. No cookies and no analytics.
